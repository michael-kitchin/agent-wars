# Grand Strategy and Hex Wargame Market: A 2025–2026 Landscape Analysis

*Prepared March 2026*

---

The strategy wargame market is thriving but deeply fragmented, with Paradox Interactive dominating grand strategy through live-service DLC models while Slitherine/Matrix Games controls the hex wargame niche. The most significant gap in the entire market is AI opponent quality — no commercial game uses LLM-based opponents despite universal player frustration, and the military sector is years ahead of commercial gaming in this area. Meanwhile, the open-source-to-paid-Steam pipeline (proven by Dwarf Fortress's $19.9 million in gross revenue) and web technology's surprising suitability for turn-based strategy create a genuine opening for new entrants.

---

## The AAA Grand Strategy Landscape Is Dominated by Paradox but Showing Cracks

Hearts of Iron IV remains the undisputed king of grand strategy wargaming, averaging 35,000 concurrent players on Steam with an all-time peak of 93,196 in November 2024. Despite launching in 2016, the game continues to grow through aggressive DLC — the "No Compromise, No Surrender" expansion ($29.99) shipped in November 2025, with a full Expansion Pass 2 underway. No Hearts of Iron V has been announced, signaling Paradox views HOI4 as a long-term platform. Crusader Kings III holds steady at roughly 18,000 average concurrent, making it Paradox's second-strongest title.

Europa Universalis V launched November 2025 to a mixed reception — 73% positive on Steam from roughly 26,000 reviews, with its first major patch "Rossbach" arriving March 2026. Player counts settled to roughly 8,000 concurrent after a 77,000-player launch peak, while EU4 stubbornly maintains roughly 8,000 concurrent players of its own with 88% positive reviews. Victoria 3 continues its slow rehabilitation, with the "Charters of Commerce" DLC sparking a comeback to 26,000 peak players in mid-2025.

The biggest headline is Civilization VII's disastrous reception — 47% positive on Steam with recent reviews dipping to 38% "Mostly Negative." The controversial Ages system that forces civilization changes alienated the core audience. Player counts dropped below 5,000 concurrent by May 2025, with both Civilization V and VI surpassing Civ VII in active players. This represents the largest stumble in the 4X genre's history and creates genuine opportunity for alternatives.

Command: Modern Operations occupies a unique position as both a commercial game ($79.99 on Steam) and a professional military tool used by 150+ defense clients across 22 countries including the US DoD, NATO, Boeing, and Lockheed Martin. Its commercial player base is tiny (single-digit to low-double-digit concurrent) but its influence is outsized, and the "Fail Safe" DLC shipped in February 2025.

## Hex Wargames Thrive in a Passionate but Tiny Niche

The hex wargame market is small in raw player counts but remarkably healthy in engagement and loyalty. Unity of Command II (2019, $29.99) is widely considered the modern gold standard — 91% positive reviews, praised for its supply-line mechanics and challenging AI. The complete bundle with 10 DLC campaigns offers hundreds of hours of content.

Gary Grigsby's War in the East/West series represents the deep end of the pool. Both titles command $79.99 price points with 90% positive reviews, but player counts hover in the single digits to low twenties concurrent. The planned "War in Europe" mega-map of 134,000 hexes (378×355) would be unprecedented in scale. These games demonstrate that the grognard audience will pay premium prices for extreme depth.

Strategic Command has quietly built one of the strongest reputations in the genre — the WWII: War in Europe entry holds 91% positive reviews, and the series now spans WWI, WWII (multiple theaters), and the American Civil War. The publisher Fury Software continues to expand through Matrix/Slitherine with a War in the Pacific entry in 2024 and new DLC through 2025.

Hex of Steel is the breakout indie story: a solo French developer (Valentin Lievre) created a WWII hex wargame that achieved 95% "Overwhelmingly Positive" from roughly 740 reviews on Steam at just $14.99–24.99. Over 400 updates, cross-platform multiplayer, and a level editor drove sustained community engagement. This is the clearest proof that a solo developer can compete in the hex wargame space.

For Axis & Allies specifically, Axis & Allies 1942 Online by Beamdog is the primary digital adaptation, available on Steam with roughly 5,000 reviews. The open-source TripleA engine provides a free alternative for A&A-style gameplay. The physical board game line is now published by Renegade Game Studios, with "Stalingrad" ($80) shipping August 2025.

Slitherine/Matrix Games is the dominant specialist publisher with an enormous catalog spanning Panzer Corps 2, Flashpoint Campaigns, Order of Battle (the genre's only notable free-to-play hex wargame), Armored Brigade II, Rule the Waves 3, and the entire Combat Mission franchise (acquired in 2024). Their 2026 lineup includes Bolt Action, RuneQuest: Warlords, and Stormbinders.

## AI Opponents Remain Strategy Gaming's Greatest Unsolved Problem

No commercially released strategy game uses LLMs as the primary AI opponent decision engine. This is the single largest gap in the market. Every major strategy game relies on scripted decision trees with numeric bonuses at higher difficulties, and player frustration is universal. CivFanatics forums summarize the sentiment bluntly: "Strong AI is obviously not a priority of the Civilization franchise." Hearts of Iron IV's AI is widely criticized for bizarre strategic decisions, and the pattern repeats across every Paradox title — difficulty comes from resource bonuses rather than smarter play.

The military sector is years ahead of commercial gaming. Johns Hopkins APL's GenWar Lab (announced October 2025) integrates LLMs with the AFSIM military simulation framework, allowing any player position to be human or AI-driven. The U.S. Air Force's WarMatrix program seeks AI-powered wargames running 10,000x faster than real-time. The U.S. Army War College used LLMs in their "Pacific Strategy" wargame with "universally positive results."

Research consistently shows LLMs are strong strategists but poor tacticians. Meta's CICERO achieved top-10% human performance in Diplomacy through a combination of a 2.7B parameter language model and strategic planning. Independent experiments by Alex Duffy pitting 12 different LLMs against each other in Diplomacy found that the models that performed best learned to lie, deceive, and betray. Baptiste Alloui-Cros's detailed analysis concluded LLMs have near-zero tactical competency but strong high-level strategic reasoning — what he described as high-altitude thinking akin to politicians discussing matters they don't fully understand.

All commercial AI middleware (NVIDIA ACE, Inworld AI, Convai) focuses exclusively on conversational NPCs, not strategic decision-making. No commercial tool exists specifically for strategy game AI. No game offers a "bring your own AI" configuration despite strong community interest — a Manifold Markets prediction tracks when LLMs will beat Paradox's in-game AI, highlighting unfilled demand. The hybrid architecture of LLMs for strategic reasoning combined with reinforcement learning for tactical execution appears to be the most promising path, but no commercial product has implemented it.

## Indie Strategy Games Succeed Through Early Access and Passionate Communities

Hooded Horse has emerged as the defining indie strategy publisher, selling 4.5 million copies across its catalog in 2024 alone. Their model — no recoup, 65% developer revenue share, no institutional investors — has attracted titles like Manor Lords (2.5M copies), Against the Storm (1.2M copies, Overwhelmingly Positive), and Old World (350,000 copies). Their 2025 sales matched 2024 at 4.5 million copies but more evenly distributed across the catalog.

Old World (Mohawk Games, $39.99) is the textbook indie grand strategy success story. Designed by Soren Johnson of Civilization IV fame, it used Epic exclusivity to fund development, then launched on Steam with Hooded Horse as publisher. Over 300,000 copies sold with 84% positive reviews, a Grammy-nominated soundtrack, and strong dynasty/narrative mechanics that differentiated it from Civilization rather than competing head-on. This is key — every direct "Civ killer" attempt (Humankind, Millennia at 68% positive, Ara: History Untold at 69–71%) achieved only mixed reception.

Terra Invicta (Pavonis Interactive/Hooded Horse) spent three years in Early Access before hitting 1.0 in January 2026 with 87% positive reviews. The team's Long War mod pedigree built credibility from day one. Shadow Empire, created by a single developer (Victor Reijkersz), launched at $39.99 and achieved 87% positive reviews through sheer depth of simulation.

Nearly every successful indie strategy title follows the Early Access pipeline: build community during EA through Discord and YouTube content creators, iterate based on feedback, then launch 1.0 to a pre-built audience. Team sizes range from 1 person (Shadow Empire, Hex of Steel) to 15–25 (Old World). Technology stacks cluster around Unity, with some custom engines.

Price sensitivity in this market skews higher than gaming overall. The sweet spot is $30–40 for serious strategy games. Wargame audiences tend to be older and more affluent, willing to pay $40+ for quality. Slitherine's $60–80 pricing works for the most dedicated grognards. The $15 tier (Hex of Steel) drives volume and high review scores but requires minimal operating costs.

Community building follows consistent patterns. Discord is the primary hub, followed by niche YouTubers (DasTactic, The Avid Wargamer) whose coverage can make or break a wargame launch. Reddit communities (r/4Xgaming, r/wargames, r/computerwargames) serve as discovery engines. Steam Workshop and mod support dramatically extend game longevity.

## Web Technologies Are Surprisingly Viable for Turn-Based Strategy

Screeps is the gold standard for JavaScript-based strategy games on Steam — 1,887 reviews at 86% positive, with its entire engine, server, and renderer open-sourced on GitHub. The game runs on Node.js, proving the full web stack can support a commercially successful strategy title.

The conventional wisdom that Electron is unsuitable for games is correct for performance-intensive titles but misleading for turn-based strategy. As one developer noted, for people building a UI-heavy game with lots of menus and text, the web toolkit is leagues better than anything in the game engine space. Turn-based hex wargames are uniquely well-suited to web technologies because they don't require 60fps rendering, they're fundamentally UI/data-driven, and web's layout and styling capabilities exceed what Unity or Unreal offer for complex information displays.

Established pipelines exist for shipping web games to Steam via Electron — Phaser.io published an official guide in March 2025, and multiple developers have shipped titles this way. Frameworks like Phaser (HTML5 game framework), PixiJS (2D WebGL renderer), and Excalibur.js (TypeScript game engine) all support Electron packaging. Performance concerns can be mitigated through WebAssembly for compute-heavy operations, worker threads for parallel processing, and native code via Node-API. Tauri (Rust backend, smaller footprint) is emerging as a lighter alternative to Electron.

The legitimate downsides are roughly 100MB minimum Chromium overhead, stigma in gaming communities ("it's just a website in a wrapper"), and Steam Input controller support issues since Electron 27+. For a hex wargame where the core interaction is clicking hexes and reading information panels, these are manageable trade-offs rather than dealbreakers.

## The Open-Source-to-Steam Model Is Proven and Lucrative

Dwarf Fortress is the definitive case study. After 16 years as a free ASCII download, the paid Steam version ($29.99) with pixel graphics sold 160,000 copies in 24 hours and exceeded 1 million copies by April 2025, generating an estimated $19.9 million in gross revenue. Monthly income jumped from roughly $15,000 in donations to $7.2 million in January 2023 alone. When asked if players had been waiting 25 years for a chance to pay, over 3,000 people responded "Yes."

Other successful examples include Cataclysm: Dark Days Ahead (free on GitHub, paid Steam version launched 2023), Battle for Wesnoth (open-source since 2003, free on Steam with 94% positive from 3,086 reviews), Mindustry (free on itch.io, paid on Steam), and Shapez.io (open-source, paid on Steam). The "pay for convenience" model works when the Steam version adds auto-updates, Workshop integration, cloud saves, achievements, and streamlined installation.

Steam's discovery algorithm presents challenges for niche games. The platform hosts 832+ games with the "Hex Grid" tag — large enough for a market but requiring careful positioning. Specific sub-genre tags dramatically outperform generic ones. Click-through rates below 0.5% mean burial; above 3% triggers active promotion. Steam Next Fest demos, concentrated wishlist velocity bursts, and niche content creator partnerships are the proven discovery strategies. Valve has stated there are "no black marks" from poor launches — games that find their audience later can still succeed algorithmically.

## WEGO Mechanics Are Niche but Respected, with Renewed Investment

The simultaneous turn-resolution (WEGO) system remains a niche mechanic found almost exclusively in dedicated wargames. The biggest news is Combat Mission 3 — following Slitherine's 2024 acquisition of Battlefront.com, a completely new Unity-based engine is in development with both WWII and modern titles planned. The CM2 engine's final release (Cold War: British Army of the Rhine DLC) shipped September 2025.

Dominions 6 (January 2024, $44.99) demonstrates WEGO's devoted following: a 91/100 player score on Steambase with a remarkable 22.5% 100-day retention rate (most games achieve 2–5%). Peak concurrent players hit roughly 1,666 at launch, settling to 163–295 — small numbers that mask extraordinarily deep engagement. The explicitly named WEGO WWII series launched two operational-level titles in 2025 (Stalingrad in June, Overlord in September).

Frozen Synapse, once the poster child for tactical WEGO, is effectively in maintenance mode. Frozen Synapse Prime was delisted from Steam in 2024. Battlestar Galactica Deadlock, a well-regarded 3D WEGO space combat game, was also delisted in November 2025.

Community sentiment is clear: WEGO works best at operational and strategic abstraction levels where the planning phase maps naturally to real command decisions. At tactical levels with individual unit actions, it becomes micromanagement-heavy. WEGO also creates specific AI challenges — the AI must commit to plans without reacting mid-execution, making scripted opponents even weaker than in IGO-UGO systems. This makes WEGO games a particularly strong candidate for improved AI approaches.

## Kilometer-Resolution Maps Push Against Hard Technical Limits

Hearts of Iron IV's province system maxes out at roughly 19,000–22,000 provinces before the engine crashes — the map uses BMP bitmaps where each pixel represents a province at base resolution. The game ships with roughly 13,000 provinces, varying from a few kilometers across in dense European areas to enormous regions in Siberia. Late-game performance degradation is well-documented as supply chains, AI decisions, and combat calculations scale with province count.

Gary Grigsby's series operates at 10 miles (16 km) per hex, with War in the East spanning roughly 25,000 hexes and War in the West roughly 36,000 hexes. Each hex tracks terrain type, road quality, rail networks, dynamic weather, and supply costs. AI turn processing at this scale is notoriously slow — full turns can take minutes. The planned War in Europe mega-map at 134,000 hexes would represent the extreme frontier.

Command: Modern Operations takes the most ambitious approach, using actual latitude/longitude coordinates with tile-based map layers streaming satellite imagery (Sentinel-2) and topographic data on demand — functionally a military Google Earth. The game acknowledged that there's no way to include the whole globe at high resolution due to licensing and data size, so it streams tiles at varying detail levels. This tile-streaming architecture, borrowed from web mapping services, is the most technically sophisticated solution to the global-map problem.

The documented technical challenges form a consistent pattern across all these games. Pathfinding scales poorly (hierarchical A* is the standard mitigation). AI decision quality degrades as map complexity increases. Memory management becomes critical with hundreds of thousands of data-bearing hexes. Micromanagement overwhelms players without automation tools. Map projection distortion, data licensing complexity (Stamen Maps went paid in 2023, disrupting CMO), and the fundamental tension between geographic realism and gameplay pacing round out the challenge set.

## Conclusion: Where the Gaps and Opportunities Lie

The market data reveals several clear structural gaps. First, AI opponent quality is the largest unaddressed problem in strategy gaming — universal player frustration, no LLM integration in any commercial product, and the military sector demonstrating feasibility years ahead of the games industry. A strategy game with genuinely intelligent AI would have no competition. Second, the open-source-to-paid-Steam pipeline is proven ($19.9M for Dwarf Fortress) but no hex wargame has fully exploited it. Third, web/Electron technology is technically viable for turn-based strategy and offers development speed advantages, but no notable wargame has shipped this way — creating a first-mover narrative opportunity. Fourth, WEGO mechanics combined with improved AI could be transformative, since WEGO's planning phase maps naturally to LLM-style reasoning about strategic decisions. The audience is small (800+ hex games on Steam) but intensely loyal, older, willing to pay $30–40, and starved for innovation in AI and interface design.
